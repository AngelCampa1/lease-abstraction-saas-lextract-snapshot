An estoppel certificate is a signed statement by a tenant (or landlord) that certifies specific facts about a lease -- rent amounts, lease status, the existence of any defaults or claims, and the completeness of the lease document set. Estoppel certificates are also referred to as certificates of estoppel - the terms are used interchangeably in commercial real estate practice. The word "estoppel" comes from the legal doctrine that prevents a party from later asserting facts inconsistent with what they have already certified under estoppel.

In plain terms: once you sign an estoppel, you are legally bound by what it says. If the estoppel contains errors, you may be estopped from asserting the correct facts later. That makes accuracy the non-negotiable requirement for every estoppel certificate.

## When Estoppel Certificates Are Required

Estoppels arise in three primary contexts:

**Property acquisitions.** When a building is sold, the buyer requires estoppel certificates from all tenants before closing. The buyer is relying on the certified facts to confirm that the leases are in full force and effect, that there are no undisclosed modifications, and that the tenants have no pending claims against the landlord that would survive the sale. The purchase price and the buyer's financing are typically conditioned on satisfactory estoppel certificates.

**Mortgage refinancing.** Lenders providing financing against a commercial property require tenant estoppels as a condition of closing. The lender needs confirmation that the leases generating the property's income are valid, unmodified (except as disclosed), and that no material defaults exist.

**SNDA agreements.** Subordination, Non-Disturbance, and Attornment agreements are often executed simultaneously with estoppel certificates. The estoppel confirms current lease facts; the SNDA establishes the relationship between the lease and the new financing.

Most commercial leases require tenants to provide estoppel certificates within 10 to 30 days of a landlord request. Failing to deliver on time -- or delivering a materially deficient estoppel -- can constitute a tenant default under the lease, and some leases authorize the landlord to execute the estoppel on behalf of the tenant if the tenant does not respond in time.

## What an Estoppel Certificate Certifies

A standard commercial estoppel certificate typically certifies 12 items:

1. **Lease identification.** The lease is identified by date, landlord, tenant, and premises description. All amendments are identified by date and title.

2. **Lease commencement and expiration.** The certified commencement date and the current expiration date, including any exercised renewal periods.

3. **Current rent.** The current monthly base rent, confirmed as of the estoppel date. Any upcoming scheduled increases are identified.

4. **Rent status.** Rent has been paid through a stated date. No rent has been paid more than one month in advance (protecting the buyer from the landlord pocketing advance payments that would reduce future collections).

5. **Security deposit.** The amount of security deposit held by the landlord, and whether any portion has been applied or returned.

6. **No defaults.** To the best of the tenant's knowledge, the landlord is not in default under the lease, and the tenant is not in default. No events have occurred that, with notice or the passage of time, would constitute a default.

7. **No pending claims.** The tenant has no claims, defenses, or rights of offset against the landlord. No free rent, credits, or allowances are outstanding except as specifically disclosed.

8. **Lease is in full force.** The lease has not been terminated, surrendered, or modified except as identified in the estoppel.

9. **Complete document set.** The lease documents identified in the estoppel constitute the entire agreement between the parties regarding the premises. There are no side letters, oral agreements, or other modifications not reflected in the identified documents.

10. **Options.** All renewal options, expansion rights, rights of first offer, and purchase options are identified, including whether any have been exercised or expired.

11. **TI allowances and landlord obligations.** All tenant improvement allowances have been received (or the outstanding amount is specified). All landlord obligations to perform construction or deliver improvements have been satisfied (or outstanding obligations are specified).

12. **Hazardous materials.** The tenant is not using the premises for storage or handling of hazardous materials in violation of applicable law (for industrial and retail leases).

## Estoppel Certificate Template: What to Include

A complete commercial lease estoppel certificate should confirm the following items. Tenants should verify each point against their actual lease before signing:

- **Lease identification:** Full legal names of landlord and tenant, property address, suite or unit number, lease date, and all amendments by date
- **Term:** Commencement date, expiration date, and any renewal options (stating whether they have been exercised)
- **Rent:** Current monthly base rent, next scheduled escalation date and amount, whether rent has been paid through a specific date
- **Security deposit:** Amount held, whether any portion has been applied or returned
- **Defaults:** Whether landlord or tenant is currently in default, and whether any notice of default has been sent or received
- **Modifications:** Whether the lease has been modified in any way not reflected in the written amendments attached
- **Pending claims:** Whether the tenant has any pending claims against the landlord for TI reimbursement, rent credits, or any other obligation
- **Assignments:** Whether the tenant has assigned the lease or sublet any portion of the premises
- **Purchase options:** Whether any ROFR, ROFO, or purchase option has been exercised or waived

Tenants should review the landlord's proposed form carefully before signing. Lenders' forms are typically drafted in the lender's favor - they may include representations about matters the tenant has not independently verified (e.g., that no claims exist against the landlord) that could be problematic to certify. Request modifications to any representation you cannot confirm as accurate.

## Common Traps in Estoppel Preparation

**Certifying facts you have not verified.** The most dangerous mistake in estoppel preparation is signing a certificate without verifying each certified fact against the actual lease documents. Certifying that the lease document set is complete without checking against the lease file, or certifying no defaults without reviewing landlord performance obligations, exposes the tenant to estoppel-based claims if errors surface later.

**Missing amendments.** Commercial leases frequently have multiple amendments -- a first amendment for an expansion, a second amendment for a lease extension, a third for a modification of the rent schedule. If the estoppel identifies only the original lease without all amendments, the tenant has certified an incomplete document set. In a subsequent dispute, the buyer or lender could argue that the tenant is estopped from asserting any right created by an unidentified amendment.

**Overlooking outstanding landlord obligations.** If the landlord has an unfulfilled obligation -- a pending TI reimbursement, a warranty repair, a parking space the landlord has not yet delivered -- and the tenant signs an estoppel stating that all landlord obligations have been performed, that claim is gone. Review landlord obligations carefully before certifying completion.

**Signing under time pressure without review.** Estoppels frequently arrive with tight turnaround requests -- sometimes 5 to 10 days -- as part of a transaction timeline. Signing quickly to avoid being the obstacle in a deal is understandable but risky. If the review reveals discrepancies, respond with a qualified estoppel that preserves your position rather than a clean estoppel that waives it.

**Conflating "to the best of knowledge" and absolute certification.** Most estoppels include a "to the best of tenant's knowledge" qualifier for certain representations. Understand which certifications are absolute and which are knowledge-qualified, and review accordingly.

## How to Prepare an Estoppel From a Lease Abstract

An accurate lease abstract makes estoppel preparation efficient and reliable. The abstract should contain each item that an estoppel will certify:

- All lease documents with dates (master lease and every amendment)
- Commencement and expiration dates
- Current base rent and escalation schedule
- Security deposit amount and status
- All options with their terms and exercise status
- Outstanding landlord obligations and TI allowance status

With this data in hand, the estoppel preparation process becomes a verification exercise: compare each certificate item to the abstract, confirm the abstract against the lease documents, and certify only what the documents support.

For any item where the abstract data and the actual documents do not align, resolve the discrepancy before signing. The estoppel is signed under oath in some jurisdictions -- accuracy is not optional.

## Estoppel Certificate vs. SNDA

Estoppel certificates and SNDAs (Subordination, Non-Disturbance, and Attornment Agreements) are both commonly requested in the same transaction but serve distinct purposes.

An **estoppel certificate** is a unilateral tenant certification of current lease status. The tenant confirms facts - rent amount, commencement date, absence of defaults - and those facts bind the tenant going forward. It is a representation, not a contract.

An **SNDA** is a three-party contract between landlord, tenant, and lender that establishes the hierarchy of interests and governs what happens to the tenant's lease if the lender forecloses on the property. The "subordination" clause confirms the lease is subordinate to the lender's mortgage; the "non-disturbance" clause protects the tenant from eviction if they are not in default; the "attornment" clause requires the tenant to recognize a new owner after foreclosure.

Both documents are typically requested simultaneously in a financing or sale transaction. The estoppel confirms current lease status for underwriting; the SNDA protects the parties' rights in a future default scenario. For a detailed breakdown, see [SNDA agreements explained](/resources/articles/snda-agreements-explained).

## What Happens if You Sign an Inaccurate Estoppel

The consequences of an inaccurate estoppel depend on who relies on it and how.

A buyer who purchases a property in reliance on a tenant estoppel that misstates rent amounts, defaults, or the existence of a pending claim may have a direct claim against the signing tenant based on the tenant's estoppel representations. The estoppel-based claim may survive the lease and continue after the tenant vacates.

Courts have consistently held tenants estopped from asserting facts inconsistent with a signed estoppel certificate, even when the error was unintentional. The legal doctrine applies regardless of subjective intent -- if you certified it, you are bound by it.

Preparing estoppels from a complete, verified lease abstract is not just an administrative best practice. It is the primary protection against signing away contractual rights you did not intend to waive.
